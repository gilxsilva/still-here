We use the SSA 2023 period life table ↗, as used in the 2026 Trustees Report. Retrieved October 18, 2026.

It covers the U.S.-based Social Security area population. See the source for its full population definition. We use the row for age at your last birthday, from 18 to 100.

The female and male tables are shown together so we can begin with age alone. The range is a comparison of groups, not an uncertainty interval.

This is a student project inspired by CS109. It is not affiliated with Stanford or the SSA.

A complement, a chain rule, a root.

Let q be the probability of dying between exact ages x and x + 1, given survival to x. Let p be a constant daily conditional probability of dying in that age-year.

01 · Complement P(survive the year) = 1 − q

02 · Conditional chain rule (1 − p)365 = 1 − q

03 · Solve for a day p = 1 − (1 − q)1/365

Each day’s survival is conditional on reaching that day. This does not assume independent lifetimes. We implement the root as −expm1(log1p(−q) / 365) for precision.

CS109: conditional probability and the chain rule ↗

An average is not an expiry date.

The daily estimate keeps risk constant within an age-year and uses 365 days. It leaves out health, behavior, seasons, leap years, and when your next birthday falls. “Tomorrow” means the next 24 hours, conditional on being alive now.

The weeks view uses the table’s remaining life expectancy at your current age, eₓ, multiplied by 52 and rounded. It does not subtract your age from life expectancy at birth. Years lived are approximated as age × 52. This is an illustration, not a dated life calendar.

The grid compares two means under fixed 2023 mortality rates. Future rates may change. Individual lives can be shorter or longer than either mean.

The weekly sliders are editable planning assumptions, not population averages. Count each hour once.
